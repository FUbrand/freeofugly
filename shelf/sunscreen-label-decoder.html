Sunscreen for men: how to read the label

Short answerThe label terms that matter are broad spectrum, SPF 30 or higher, and water resistant with a stated time. Words like waterproof, sweatproof and sunblock are not allowed on US sunscreens.

The full answer

  • Broad Spectrum: the FDA says passing the broad spectrum test shows the product provides UVA protection proportional to its UVB protection. Sunscreens that are not broad spectrum, and broad spectrum ones with SPF 2 to 14, can only claim to help prevent sunburn.
  • Water resistant: the label must say whether the product stays effective for 40 minutes or 80 minutes while swimming or sweating. Sunscreens that are not water resistant must carry a direction to use a water resistant one if swimming or sweating.
  • Banned claims: sunscreens cannot be labelled "waterproof" or "sweatproof", or identified as "sunblocks", because those claims overstate effectiveness. They also cannot claim more than 2 hours of protection without reapplication, or immediate protection, without submitting data and getting FDA approval.
  • SPF 30 or higher: this is the AAD's recommendation, together with broad spectrum and water resistant.
  • How much: the AAD says at least 1 teaspoon for your face, and about 1 ounce (a shot glass) for the rest of exposed skin for most adults. Reapply every two hours, and right after swimming or sweating.

What this does not tell you

  • The FDA page we read is the Q&A on the 2011 final rule. It carries a content-current date of 10/22/2014. We did not find a newer FDA page replacing these label terms.
  • In December 2025 the FDA proposed adding a new sunscreen ingredient, bemotrizinol, to the monograph. That is about an ingredient, not these label terms. We could not confirm from our search whether a final order has been issued, so check the FDA's OTC monograph portal for the current status.
  • A label tells you what a product is allowed to claim, not how it feels or how well you will wear it.
