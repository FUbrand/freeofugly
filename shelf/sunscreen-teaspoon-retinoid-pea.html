The Verdict

Sunscreen: use more than you think. Retinoid: use less.

Short answerMost people put on far less sunscreen than it was tested with, so the SPF on the bottle overstates what they get. Dermatologists say at least a teaspoon for your face. A retinoid works the other way: a thin layer over the whole area, once a day at most. Extra does not work faster. It only irritates.

Sunscreen: the amount most guys skip

The American Academy of Dermatology, the professional body for US dermatologists, says to use at least 1 teaspoon of sunscreen on your face, about the length of your index and middle fingers, and to reapply every two hours outdoors.

SPF is the sun protection number on the bottle. It is measured in the lab at 2 milligrams of sunscreen per square centimeter of skin. Studies of how people really apply it find about a quarter of that. A 2007 Danish study of SPF and sunscreen amount (Faurschou and Wulf) reports that people apply roughly a quarter of the tested amount.

What a quarter of the amount does: in one study funded by Neutrogena (Ou-Yang and colleagues, 2012), products labeled SPF 70 and SPF 100 measured SPF 19 and SPF 27 when applied at a quarter of the tested amount. That is about 27 percent of the label number both times. Apply the same ratio to an SPF 50 and you get roughly 12 to 14. In plain words: in those lab tests, a quarter of the sunscreen gave roughly a quarter of the SPF.

Try this tonight: squeeze 1 teaspoon of your sunscreen into a measuring spoon once. Look at how much that is. Then put it on your face, neck and ears. Most people are surprised by the amount.

One wording note. "A teaspoon" is the dermatologists' guidance. The lab test amount works out smaller: about a quarter teaspoon for the face alone and about half a teaspoon for face and neck together. Either way, it is far more than most people use.

Retinoid: the amount most guys overdo

A retinoid is a vitamin A skin treatment. The one sold over the counter for acne is adapalene (Differin). Its drug label says to cover the entire affected area with a thin layer, use it once a day, and not more than once a day. It also says: "Applying more than directed will not provide faster or better results, but may worsen skin irritation."

That means the whole area, not single pimples. A pea-sized amount is the usual rule of thumb for spreading over a whole face. The label itself says "a thin layer", not a size.

Expect a rough start. The label says your acne may appear to worsen before it improves, and calls that normal. Do not judge it in the first couple of weeks.

Try this tonight: a pea-sized amount, spread thin over your whole face, once. If your skin gets dry or red, use it less often, not more.

What this does not tell you

  • The "quarter of the amount" figure is an average from studies of how people apply sunscreen. Your own amount may be higher or lower. We could not open the full text of the 2007 study, only summaries of it.
  • How fast protection falls as you use less is argued over. The 2012 study above found a drop that is close to straight-line. The 2007 study found the drop is exponential, which would be worse. A 2012 Chinese study (Liu and colleagues) found a straight-line drop for low SPF products and an exponential one for SPF 30 and 55. Other reviewers say half the amount gives about a third to a half of the label SPF, which is milder than straight-line. So "roughly 12 to 14" is the straight-line estimate, not a promise.
  • The 2012 study was paid for by Neutrogena, whose employees were among the authors. Its own conclusion was that very high SPF products make up for under-use better than SPF 30 or 50 do. These are lab results, not real days outdoors.
  • We found no single study that measures the "teaspoon for the face" figure. It is the dermatologists' recommendation, and the lab dose works out smaller. We did not find a study of which one gets better protection in real life.
  • The drug label does not use the word "pea." Pea-sized is common dermatologist advice, but we could not find it on a dermatologists' association page, so treat it as a rule of thumb. If you use a prescription retinoid, follow your doctor.
  • Sunscreen math is about SPF only. We did not compare brands, ingredients or UVA protection.
