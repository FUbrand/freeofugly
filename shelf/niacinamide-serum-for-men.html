Niacinamide serum for men: what it does and what it doesn't

Short answerNiacinamide at 5% in a moisturizer improved the look of aging facial skin in one placebo-controlled 12-week trial. Whether it does the same for men, shrinks pores, or works better at higher strengths has not been shown in the studies we found.

The full answer

The main trial we found: Bissett and colleagues, Int J Cosmet Sci, 2004. It was a 12-week, double-blind, placebo-controlled, split-face, left-right randomised study in 50 Caucasian women aged 40 to 60. One half of the face got a moisturizer containing 5% niacinamide, the other half got the same moisturizer without it. Niacinamide was well tolerated and gave significant improvements versus control in fine lines and wrinkles, hyperpigmented spots, texture and red blotchiness. Skin yellowing (sallowness) also improved.

A 2005 paper from the same group, in Dermatologic Surgery, describes a similar design (50 women, 5%, 12 weeks, split-face) and adds elasticity. Its abstract does not say whether it is a separate cohort, so we do not count it as independent evidence.

What is missing is just as useful. Neither abstract reports a pore-size result, and the 2004 trial's product was a moisturizer, not a serum (the 2005 abstract says "vehicle control"). The participants were women, so men's results are not shown. Niacinamide was not compared with sunscreen or a retinoid, so it is not a replacement for either. And only 5% was tested, so these trials do not show that 10% or 12% works better.

What this does not tell you

  • Both papers list Procter & Gamble as the authors' affiliation. The trials are placebo-controlled, but the sponsor relationship is something to weigh. We did not find a funding statement in the abstracts.
  • We read the abstracts through the PubMed record; we did not read the full text. A pore result may exist in the full paper, and we say only that it is not in the abstracts.
  • Prices vary widely. We did not find a trial linking results to price.
