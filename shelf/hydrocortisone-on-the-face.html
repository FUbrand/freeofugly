Hydrocortisone on your face: what the label and the reports say

Short answerDrugstore hydrocortisone 1% is a mild steroid, and its US label does not bar the face, but it says to stop and ask a doctor if symptoms last past 7 days. The rosacea-like rashes described in the medical literature come mostly from strong steroids used for weeks, so the risk from 1% is likely lower. Weeks of daily use on the face is still the pattern behind those cases. If you have used it that long, do not quit cold; ask a dermatologist how to come off it.

The full answer

The label on a US hydrocortisone 1% cream (ProStat First Aid, on DailyMed) lists use for temporary relief of itching from minor skin irritations and rashes, including seborrheic dermatitis. It says not to use it in the eyes or for diaper rash, and to stop use and ask a doctor if the condition worsens or lasts more than 7 days. It also says not to continue any hydrocortisone product for longer than 7 days. It does not bar use on the face.

DermNet, reviewed by dermatologist Dr Amanda Oakley (page dated June 2014), describes steroid rosacea as a rosacea-like condition on the mid-face caused by potent topical steroids or their withdrawal. Redness, bumps and pustules appear after several weeks of applying a steroid to the forehead, eyelids, cheeks or chin. It is more likely and more severe with strong steroids, and less likely with mild steroids, particularly when applied infrequently. It can flare badly when the cream is stopped, and DermNet recommends slow withdrawal.

A 2011 series of 200 patients (Bhat and colleagues) with more than a month of topical steroid use on the face found the majority were using potent (class II) steroids for trivial facial skin problems. A 2021 case report (Diehl and Cohen, Cureus) describes one man who developed a perioral and rosacea-like rash after using a high-potency steroid twice daily on the face.

So the evidence ties the problem to strong steroids and to weeks of use. We did not find a trial testing 1% hydrocortisone on the face over weeks, so we cannot say how often it happens with the drugstore strength.

What this does not tell you

  • All of the rash evidence here is case reports and clinic series. None is a controlled trial.
  • The case report and the series involve strong steroids, not hydrocortisone 1%.
  • The DermNet page is dated 2014.
  • Not every perioral rash is caused by steroids; some occur with no steroid use.
  • Information, not medical advice. If you have a face rash that keeps coming back, see a dermatologist before you stop or restart any steroid.
